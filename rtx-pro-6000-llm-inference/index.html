What I learned

  • Hard coding quality: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 (2-bit) > Qwen3.6-27B > Inkling > Qwen3.6-35B-A3B on the 40 hard LiveCodeBench v6 problems. DeepSeek beats Inkling 9–1 (p = 0.02) and the 35B Qwen 9–0 on problems only one solved; against Qwen3.6-27B it is 5–1 (p = 0.22, suggestive only).
  • Split the cards with tensor parallelism. TP2 beats pipeline parallel by ~40% on DeepSeek even with no NVLink (the all-reduce is ~1 ms of a ~10 ms step), and expert parallelism changes nothing at one to four users.
  • Speculative decoding is the biggest lever for one user (2–3× at batch 1) and it does not change accuracy.
  • Evaluation settings swing results by ~20 points. Greedy decoding with a 20K-token cap understated DeepSeek (62.5% → 82.5%) and Inkling (40% → 62.5%). Use each vendor's recommended sampling and give reasoning models 100K+ tokens.
  • V4.1-Flash-1017 is not small: 763B parameters (552B backbone + 196B Engram memory). The official NVFP4 build is 429 GB and cannot fit in 192 GB, so it runs as a 2-bit GGUF (138 GB backbone on the GPUs, 110 GB Engram on SSD) in llama.cpp, not SGLang.
  • Setup gotchas on RTX PRO 6000 (SM120): DeepSeek on SGLang needs --moe-runner-backend flashinfer_cutlass plus a one-file patch for the draft head; the default kernels only exist for B200-class GPUs.

Gotchas

  • The lmsysorg/sglang:inkling-2-mini image is not plain upstream: it carries SM120-only kernels. Don't swap in an upstream checkout.
  • DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 on SGLang needs --moe-runner-backend flashinfer_cutlass (the auto-picked TRT-LLM kernels only ship for B200-class GPUs) and a one-line change so the DSpark draft head's MXFP4 experts use the SM120 CUTLASS path.
  • DSpark draft length is fixed by the checkpoint (8 for Inkling, 6 for DeepSeek-0731); it cannot be tuned.
  • llama.cpp must be at least b11870 for DeepSeek-V4.1 (the image above is b12602). The 110 GB Engram file is memory-mapped from SSD; keep it on NVMe and avoid memory-hungry neighbours on a 125 GB host.
  • The 1017 download is 249 GB; a wired connection saves hours.

5 · How far to trust this

  • Small samples. 40 hard problems with one sample at temperature 1.0 gives intervals of roughly ±12–15 points per model; only the DeepSeek-vs-Inkling and DeepSeek-vs-Qwen35B gaps are statistically clear.
  • Contamination. LiveCodeBench v6 covers contests up to April 2025, likely inside every model's training data. HumanEval and MBPP are older still.
  • Not agentic. Nothing here measures repo-level work (SWE-bench style), long context quality, or tool use beyond a 15-prompt check.
  • Vendor claims are vendor claims. The 2-bit quantization's parity with the base model comes from its authors; we only tested what is on this page.
  • Uneven timing. DeepSeek ran one request at a time while the others ran four in parallel, so compare tokens per problem and single-stream speed rather than wall time.

1. Setup that worked

  • Hardware: 2× NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell Max-Q (SM120, 96 GB each), no NVLink; nvidia-smi topo -p2p r shows P2P OK. AMD EPYC host (48 threads, 125 GB RAM).
  • Software: Docker image lmsysorg/sglang:inkling-2-mini (sglang 03fde9cc3 + SM120 kernels, torch 2.14.1+cu130, FlashInfer 0.7.4 with sm120f kernels). Note the image is not upstream: it carries SM120-only kernels that upstream lacks, so don't swap in an upstream checkout.
  • Launch scripts (tested end to end): deploy/serve_inkling.sh {tp2|replicas|gpu0|gpu1|stop}, deploy/serve_deepseek.sh [stop] (SGLang, V4-Flash-0731), deploy/serve_deepseek41_1017.sh {full|coder|stop} (llama.cpp, V4.1-Flash-1017).
  • Only one patch was needed (deploy/patches/sm120_draft_moe.diff, 1 file): the DeepSeek DSpark draft head hard-wired an SM100-only TRT-LLM MoE kernel for its MXFP4 layers; on SM120 it now uses the FlashInfer CUTLASS MXFP8×MXFP4 path (already present in the image for the non-hybrid path).
  • DeepSeek on SM120 requires --moe-runner-backend flashinfer_cutlass. The auto-selected flashinfer_trtllm_routed crashes (cubins exist only for SM100/103/107 — the NVIDIA model card was validated on B200).
  • DSpark draft length is fixed by the checkpoint (num_draft_tokens = gamma+1: 8 for Inkling, 6 for DeepSeek) – not tunable.
  • Method note: perf/kernel work was started on --load-format dummy weights while the 164 GB download ran (the download was Wi-Fi limited at 15–20 MB/s for hours; plugging in Ethernet would help). All headline numbers below were re-measured on real weights.

DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731-NVFP4 (weights ≈ 79 GB/GPU in TP2)

Step latency, random tokens, 1024 in / 256 out (dummy weights – step cost does not depend on weight values):

config conc 1 ms/token (tok/s) conc 4 ms/token (tok/s total)
TP2 9.74 (98.6) 10.37 (351)

Same ordering on real weights, real prompts (HumanEval first 40, no thinking, aggregate output tok/s):

config conc 1 conc 4
TP2 96.4 262
PP2 69.0 180
TP2 + DSpark 284.0 470
  • PP2 is slower because a single stream traverses the GPUs one after the other (one GPU's bandwidth), and PP only helps once the batch is large enough to fill both stages.
  • TP2 over PCIe is not communication-bound: the custom all-reduce costs ~10 µs/call, ~1 ms of a ~10 ms step; that is why EP2 (fewer all-reduces) changes nothing.
  • Per-token time is nearly flat from batch 1→4, i.e. a step is kernel-latency-bound, which is exactly why speculative decoding (verify several tokens per step) is so effective.
  • DSpark mean accepted length on real code prompts: 4.3 tokens/step.

5. Where the time goes (DeepSeek TP2 decode, profile)

GPU is kernel-bound, not launch-bound (kernel time ≈ step time): GEMM/GEMV 56% (FP8 linear ~3.2 ms, MoE grouped GEMM ~2.1 ms, bf16 small-M cuBLAS/CUTLASS-80 WMMA GEMMs ~3.7 ms of ~15 ms), attention 8%, hyper-connection 4%, router 3%, all-reduce 7%. The image already ships an SM120 low-M bf16 GEMV that only triggers for N ≥ 2048; the router/projection GEMMs with smaller N still go to the generic WMMA kernels — that, and the mhc/elementwise kernels, are the candidates if you want to push beyond the current ~10 ms/step (no change was made).

6. Recommendations

Daily coding + assistant (one user): deploy/serve_inkling.sh tp2, send {"reasoning_effort":"low"}, temperature 0 for code, always set max_tokens (Inkling can ramble at higher effort). ~450 tok/s, sub-second tool calls, the 19.5 GB of weights (~10 GB per GPU under TP2) leave ~80 GB per GPU for KV cache and other work.

Several agents / IDE + background jobs at once: deploy/serve_inkling.sh replicas (one per GPU) or run one server on GPU 0 and use GPU 1 for something else (embeddings, image/vision model, a second model).

When to switch to DeepSeek: for the hardest problems use V4.1-Flash-1017 (section 7): serve_deepseek41_1017.sh full (146 tok/s on both GPUs) – or run the Coder variant on GPU 0 (129 tok/s) and Inkling on GPU 1 (307 tok/s) at the same time, which is the best use of two cards if you code most of the day and still want a fast assistant. V4-Flash-0731 (SGLang) is now mostly superseded: slower than Inkling and smaller than 1017; its non-thinking default also drops ~10 coding points (use reasoning_effort: low).

Don't bother with: PP2, EP2, or hand-tuning the draft length (fixed by the checkpoint).

Housekeeping: both servers use --restart unless-stopped; run serve_*.sh stop before launching the other model (both want the GPUs). Weights and the image are cached in ~/.cache/huggingface and Docker, so restarts take ~5–6 minutes (DeepSeek) / ~5 minutes (Inkling TP2), dominated by CUDA-graph capture and kernel autotune.

7. DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 (the one you asked for)

What it is. deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 has a 552B-parameter backbone (384 experts/layer, 40 layers, hidden 5120) plus a 196B "Engram" lookup memory (two huge n-gram embedding tables), 763B in total, 1M context, native image input. That is roughly twice V4-Flash-0731. Official sizes: 510 GB (FP8/FP4); nvidia/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017-NVFP4 429 GB (the backbone alone is ~330 GB, so it cannot fit in 192 GB of VRAM even with Engram offloaded; NVIDIA validated it on 4× GB300). SGLang therefore cannot serve it on this machine, and neither can vLLM.

What works: llama.cpp + ISTA-DASLab "GSQ-RCO" 2-bit GGUF (ISTA-DASLab/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017-GSQ-RCO-GGUF). Their IQ2_XXS build is a 138 GB backbone (2.0 bits/weight, per-tensor mixed precision) plus the shared 110 GB Engram file, which llama.cpp memory-maps from SSD and reads one row per token, so it needs neither VRAM nor RAM. Runtime: official image ghcr.io/ggml-org/llama.cpp:server-cuda13 (build 12602; needs ≥ b11870 for DeepSeek-V4.1; your local ~/llama.cpp is b7898 and too old). It runs on SM120 out of the box and supports DSpark (--spec-type draft-dspark, draft model ggml-org/…-GGUF/dspark-…-MXFP4.gguf, 7.9 GB). Total download 249 GB; load takes 1.5–4 min.

What this says: - Easy-to-medium coding is a tie (HumanEval+, MBPP+, tool calls all within noise), so there Inkling's 3× decode speed, 10× aggregate throughput and ~25× faster prefill decide it. - On harder algorithmic problems the 2-bit DeepSeek is better: +7 points overall and +7.5 on hard, with 2.4× fewer tokens per problem. 9. Best-case protocol: max effort, vendor sampling, big token budget (+ Qwen)

Sections 3 and 8 used greedy decoding (temperature 0) and small token caps. Every vendor recommends sampling instead (Inkling's generation_config: temperature 1.0; DeepSeek: temperature 1.0, top_p 1.0, max_tokens ≥ 256K; Qwen: temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, top_k 20 for coding), and Inkling's own card warns of long reasoning loops. So this round gives each model its best shot on the 40 hard LiveCodeBench v6 problems (the ones where models differ), one sample each:

model (where it ran) sampling / effort budget pass@1 (95% CI) truncated mean tokens / problem single-stream decode
DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, 2-bit (both GPUs, llama.cpp, DSpark) T 1.0, top_p 1, effort max 150K 82.5% (70–92) 1 24.6K ~104 tok/s
Qwen3.6-27B FP8 (1 GPU, SGLang, MTP) T 0.6, top_p .95, top_k 20, thinking 100K 72.5% (57–85) 0 41.1K ~125 tok/s
Inkling-2-Mini (1 GPU, SGLang, DSpark) T 1.0, effort max 100K 62.5% (47–77) 3 37.4K ~307 tok/s
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B FP8 (1 GPU, MTP) T 0.6, top_p .95, top_k 20, thinking 100K 60.0% (45–75) 2 45.0K not measured

Paired comparison (same 40 problems; problems one model solved and the other did not; exact McNemar test): - DeepSeek vs Inkling 9–1 (p = 0.02); DeepSeek vs Qwen3.6-35B-A3B 9–0 (p < 0.01): DeepSeek is significantly better than both. - DeepSeek vs Qwen3.6-27B 5–1 (p = 0.22) and Qwen3.6-27B vs Inkling 5–1 (p = 0.22): suggestive, not significant at n = 40. - 34 of 40 problems are solved by at least one model, 20 by all four, 6 by none.

What this says: - The protocol matters as much as the model. The same models scored far lower under greedy decoding with a 20K cap on the same problems: DeepSeek 62.5% → 82.5% and Inkling 40% → 62.5%. We changed effort, sampling and budget together, so we cannot say which of the three did the work. Evals that cap reasoning at a few thousand tokens badly understate these models. - DeepSeek-1017 (2-bit) is the strongest solver and also the most token-efficient (24.6K tokens per problem against 37–45K for the others; median 15K). At its ~104 tok/s that is ~4 minutes per problem, versus ~5.5 min for Qwen3.6-27B and ~2 min for Inkling. It needs both GPUs. - Qwen3.6-27B is the best single-GPU solver (72.5%, never truncated, ~125 tok/s with MTP, 29 GB weights), but it thinks very long even on easy problems (2.3K tokens per HumanEval problem against ~450 for Inkling at low effort), so it is a poor fit for quick questions. - Inkling at max effort remains the weakest of the big three on hard problems and the only one that still loops to the cap sometimes (3/40 at 100K), but it is the fastest per token. The MoE Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (3B active) is no better here. - At temperature 1.0 DeepSeek's DSpark acceptance falls (mean 2.3 tokens/step against 3.0 greedy), so its speed drops from 146 to ~104 tok/s. llama.cpp's --spec-draft-sampling probabilistic did not help. - Caveats: 40 problems and one sample at temperature 1.0 means wide intervals (±12–15 points); results are LiveCodeBench v6 (contests up to Apr 2025, likely in training data for all models); DeepSeek ran one request at a time while the others ran four in parallel, so wall times are not comparable (use tokens per problem and single-stream speed); we did not test agentic/repo-level coding.

Updated practical advice (two RTX PRO 6000): - Quick questions, assistant, tool calls: Inkling (TP2 ~450 tok/s, or GPU 0 alone). - Hardest coding problems, when you can wait: DeepSeek-1017 2-bit on both GPUs, with reasoning_effort: "max", temperature 1.0, top_p 1.0 and a budget of 100K+ tokens. - One strong coder per GPU: Qwen3.6-27B (deploy/serve_qwen.sh 27b <gpu> <port>) next to Inkling or the DeepSeek Coder variant on the other GPU. Give reasoning models real token budgets; never judge them from a 4K cap.
